# After the GPT-6 Buzz: Revisiting Agents in the Physical World

The GPT-6 Astra launch campaign with RoboCurve swept through the robotics community a few days ago. A general-purpose model can now do a surprisingly decent job of direct control, as well as work with a VLA such as π0.5. That is exciting.

Still, however spectacular the claims about a model's physical abilities get, I keep coming back to a few things: can it work autonomously over a long horizon? Can it keep up with time in the real world? And how does it know whether it actually got the last step right? That last question is about accurate state assessment, including, but certainly not limited to, spatial understanding.

Many friends who have been working on VLM agents have been thinking about that last question for a long time. In the physical world, a robot may believe it has picked up a cup while its gripper is actually empty. If it misses that, the subsequent moving, pouring, and putting back no longer make sense. A failed grasp is usually recoverable; continuing with a mistaken understanding of the state lets the problems pile up. There is a parallel with coding in the digital world. Early on, programmers talked to coding LLMs through chatbots. Without execution and verification, generated code still needed a programmer to run and debug it before the loop could close. Once execution and verification environments could be built, objective results from intermediate steps could be fed back to the agent, allowing it to retry, correct itself, and solve problems through trial and error. We need the physical world to provide that kind of feedback too. Humans sometimes double-check through another source of information: a different viewing angle, for example, or touch. Could a model invoke another tool or use another source of evidence to make that judgment? I will leave that as an open question for now.

Back to the [GPT-6 and π0.5 report](https://anonymous-report-421.github.io/public-website/?lang=zh&view=1) that set the internet buzzing. RoboDojo is probably among the hardest simulated manipulation benchmarks around today. Before this report, the highest score on its official leaderboard was still somewhere in the thirties.

The report selected ten RoboDojo tasks. Its hybrid architecture achieved an average Score of 62.60 and a success rate of 48%. But this was a subset evaluated by the authors themselves, skewed toward tasks where π0.5 had previously struggled, with only five trials per task. Other models' results were taken from the public leaderboard and recalculated for this subset, rather than rerun with the same seeds. The size of the improvement still awaits an update to the official RoboDojo leaderboard.

Even setting aside how close the gain really is to a doubling, the result got the robotics community excited. In fact, I had long wondered why nobody had tried climbing this leaderboard with a high-level model agent plus a VLA. Now someone has. The experiments distinguish the two kinds of capability fairly clearly: GPT is much stronger on semantics, ordering, and rules; specialized control policies remain steadier on contact-rich manipulation such as tower building and forming a mahjong kong. In a separate RoboLab test focused on semantic pick-and-place tasks, GPT Direct achieved 49/50, versus 46/50 for the hybrid architecture. The initial states, budgets, and retries were not fully aligned, though, so a three-trajectory gap is not enough to conclude that adding a VLA necessarily hurts. There is also a detail that the name “Direct” can obscure: GPT outputs end-effector targets, with inverse kinematics and controllers still underneath. It has taken over more of the decisions, but it is still some distance from handling all low-level control itself. Another point worth keeping in mind is the much-discussed generalization problem of VLAs: the comparison VLA in RoboLab had not received task-specific alignment training. We therefore still do not know whether the relative strengths of VLA-plus-agent and direct large-model policies across RoboDojo and RoboLab come from the task types, the presence or absence of targeted VLA alignment, or both.

Either way, this makes me wonder where, exactly, GPT is helping. Let us look at the setup.

In the hybrid architecture, π0.5 first generates the next 50 control steps. GPT then decides whether to accept a short segment, say 15 of those 50 steps, or provide a correction of its own. It can reject the proposed motion, change the current state a little, and ask π0.5 to generate again. Perhaps part of the notorious VLA generalization problem can be addressed from the environment side. Suppose the target object is near the edge of a table, at an awkward angle, with something blocking access. Handing “pick it up” straight to a local policy may produce repeated failures. But the upper-level agent could first remove the obstruction, nudge the object somewhere easier to reach, change the viewing angle, and then hand over to the VLA. The weights have not changed. The task now looks more like something it has seen before.

We usually think of generalization as getting things right in unfamiliar states. Now there is another option: the agent can turn an unfamiliar state into a familiar one. There are limits, of course. If the robot cannot reach, lacks the strength, or simply does not possess a particular contact skill, a few words from the upper-level model will not solve that. But within the range of states it can adjust, the combined system may be able to do more than any individual local policy.

[Harness VLA](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.08448v1) uses analytical motions to reposition and approach, then hands local contact over to a frozen VLA. That is a similar attempt. It makes me think we should revisit the requirement that “low-level skills must generalize as well as the upper-level model.” A skill can have a narrower range, provided the upper level knows what it is good at and can get the state there. The authors also quickly shared impressive results from plugging GPT-6 into their system on social media. In the paper's setup, however, judgments about whether each stage is complete still depend on ground truth from the simulator. We changed that to let the model judge for itself, and GPT-6 also did very well. That suggests frontier models can already observe and understand intermediate states fairly accurately on these tasks.

The problem is that we often do not know just how accurate these manipulation and understanding abilities really are.

A cup-grasping skill may have a high success rate in isolation. But whether it grasps the rim, body, or handle makes a huge difference to the next step. All three count as “picked it up.” Some make pouring easier, some make a handoff easier, and some get in the way of whatever comes next. Stringing together skills with good individual scores does not necessarily produce a reliable long-horizon task.

What state does a skill leave the world in when it finishes? Can the next skill start from there? Will it let go if interrupted? Where is the object likely to end up after a failure? If this information never enters the tool interface, the upper level has no objective answer.

[VLAs-as-Tools](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.13119v1) aligns training with specific invocations and adds feedback during execution. Once a VLA becomes a tool, the criteria for training it change: beyond completing its own task, it needs to tell the caller what it is doing and leave things ready for the next action. The goal of training it as a “brain that can carry out a long task” gives way to training it as an executor for an individual atomic task. In fact, this is already how people approach it in quite a few industrial settings.

Contact-rich tasks remain a strength of specialized policies such as VLAs. Friction, deformation, slipping, and occluded contacts all make it difficult to deliberate slowly over images and then issue end-effector targets. But the value of a VLA extends beyond that. It can sustain a sequence of actions with a compact representation and relatively low online overhead. That execution efficiency still matters even as general-purpose models improve. Spending enormous inference compute and time on a simple motion with a model as large as GPT-6 is not practical in the physical world.

“Code as Policy” itself can also mean different things. Waiting for the model to think again every few steps has very different timing from having it first write a feedback controller that then runs quickly on the local machine. The latter still has synthesis and verification problems, but it would be wrong to treat both as “the large model personally deliberates on every control cycle.”

I think two charts in [Anthropic's robotics experiments](https://www.anthropic.com/research/claude-plays-robotics) are worth reading together: the LIBERO direct-manipulation results and the VLA-supervision results. Direct manipulation shows that improvements in the base model do begin to bring more control ability, although the gains are not strictly monotonic. VLA supervision shows the other side: an upper-level model that meddles can disrupt motions that would otherwise succeed. I previously called this a system-2 tax. Even with GPT-6, I am not yet ready to assume that tax has disappeared.

Back to the [GPT-6-plus-VLA RoboDojo test](https://anonymous-report-421.github.io/public-website/?lang=zh&view=1): GPT modified only 14.4% of the control steps in the hybrid trajectories. That does not mean π0.5 contributed the remaining 85.6% of the task's success.

A share of actions is not a share of contribution. One brief correction may be what finally gives a long sequence of subsequent actions a chance to succeed. GPT also participates in the decision at every action-segment boundary; it simply often chooses to keep the VLA's proposal. Even when it changes no action, observation and decision-making have already incurred a cost.

So I am curious: which corrections fixed a semantic goal, which adjusted the geometry, and which merely prevented an imminent error? We could branch from the same state and let one run continue with the original VLA, another change only the local instruction, another rearrange the environment before resuming, and another take direct control. That should tell us more about where the benefit comes from than the 14.4% figure alone. From this angle, an expensive GPT could also serve as a teacher first.

What is worth retaining is more than the final successful trajectory. It is when the model noticed something was wrong, what it saw, which steps it changed, and when it decided that control could go back to the VLA.

There is a connection here to [DAgger](https://arxiv.org/abs/1011.0686) and its concern with the states a policy actually encounters through execution. Learning only from smooth demonstrations can miss the places where a deployed policy repeatedly gets stuck. Of course, GPT's corrections also need to be filtered by actual outcomes. They do not become correct labels just because a bigger model produced them.

Following CaP-X, Harness VLA, VoLoAgent, and Thea, my attention has gradually shifted from “how low-level an action can the model generate?” to “how does the model stay involved in a task?” Coming from training code agents, though, I have to admit that CaP-X's sparse interaction with the physical world felt rather uninspired to me from the start.

[CaP-X](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.22435v1) already includes multiple rounds of execution feedback and visual differences, so it cannot simply be dismissed as one-shot code generation. Still, much of its focus remains on the interface for code-based control. [VoLoAgent](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.07723v1) brings in monitoring during execution: actions can run asynchronously, while the upper level observes and decides whether to continue, interrupt, or recover. That is closer to what physical tasks need than invoking a tool and waiting for it to return. VoLoAgent's Monitor step gives the agent some autonomous control, the beginnings of an agentic system. What I find lacking is that the overall plan–monitor–replan framework is still fixed by hand, rather than fully controlled by the model.

[Thea](https://arxiv.org/html/2608.11246v1) comes closer to an autonomous agentic setup. It also puts navigation, manipulation, and interaction with people into the same set of tools, so the task scope can finally move beyond tabletop manipulation. But turn-based tool invocation retains the waiting that we already know so well from coding agents. Its demos are sped up several times, sometimes by factors in the tens.

I still want something closer to the autonomy of an SWE agent: provide tools and let the model decide what to do next. “No hand-written task workflow” and “no runtime protocol” are two different things, though. Someone still has to handle which actions can run together, whether a robot has actually stopped after a call is canceled, and whether an old instruction can overwrite a newer state. Putting these responsibilities into the runtime does not prevent the model from arranging tasks freely. It seems more likely to make that freedom usable.

This is where the timing problem becomes serious.

In the GPT-6-plus-VLA RoboDojo report, the hybrid architecture used roughly 12.5 million tokens per trajectory, for only about 34 seconds of robot motion. At first glance, that is staggering. But the number needs unpacking: calculated from the [reported usage](https://anonymous-report-421.github.io/public-website/?lang=zh&view=1), around 97% of the total token count came from cached input. That does not mean each trajectory generated 12.5 million fresh tokens of thought. Actual overhead also depends on cache pricing, output volume, and how long each call took.

The evaluation setup used by existing hierarchical embodied systems lets the simulator wait while the model thinks. Those 34 seconds count only the physical time represented by the control steps, excluding the wait for model responses. In reality, thinking takes time too. The environment does not stop because you are thinking.

Experiments that freeze environmental events during deliberation can tell us the model's success rate when it has enough time. On real hardware, however, objects slip, people move, and other devices keep running. Thinking for another ten seconds may produce a better action, or it may make the action completely irrelevant. We cannot ignore that delay. This is also what motivated our [EP interaction model](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.11484).

The [Gemini Robotics ER 2 launch](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/gemini-robotics-er-2/) puts considerable emphasis on continuous video and execution monitoring. I agree with that direction. Still, the often-used description of a “1 Hz upper-level brain” needs some qualification. The [API documentation](https://ai.google.dev/gemini-api/docs/robotics-streaming) specifies video input at up to 1 FPS. Frames alone do not trigger a response, and the example heartbeat waits for the previous turn to finish. Sending one frame per second does not guarantee a completed judgment every second.

Interestingly, even regular Gemini tends toward shorter calls and more turns. [CursorBench 4.0](https://prod.cursor.com/evals) provides some support for that observation: Gemini 3.8 Flash High averages 324 steps. More steps alone do not establish better real-time performance, though. Shorter calls may bring fresh feedback sooner, or they may simply add waiting and repeated observation. Behavior in a coding harness may not transfer directly to a robot either.

Their current approach is still largely turn-based. If the reasoning and decoding for a tool-call turn have not finished, an incoming warning observation does not get in. What we want is an interaction mode that does not block at all. I wonder whether we could eventually **fully decouple thinking from action, with multiple streams running in parallel**.

We cannot simply wait for upper-level inference to get faster. Many states contain little new information, making another pass through a large model wasteful; other changes cannot wait for the next long stretch of thinking to end. We need to decide when more thought is worthwhile, when to act first, and when a new observation should interrupt an old plan that has not yet been used. Perhaps a monitoring stream like this also calls for something akin to KV-cache compression. Back when I worked on KV-cache compression, the sparsity of attention was already widely recognized. That is true of text context, and even more so of video streams: thinking about human visual experience, video should contain much more redundancy both spatially and over time. It may be worth training a perception head to detect relevant changes and sparsify or compress the video input, instead of heuristically feeding the upper-level model at 1 Hz or 2 Hz.

If we need that kind of compression, we also need evaluations that reveal which errors get missed first as monitoring is reduced. It is similar to needing evidence of whether a model has lost capability after quantization or pruning.

The lower level has real-time problems too. Physical Intelligence's [Real-Time Chunking](https://www.pi.website/research/real_time_chunking) discusses generating new actions while the old ones are still executing, then making the two segments connect. It draws attention to something easy to miss: asynchronous execution involves more than sending a request early. By the time the new policy takes over, the robot has reached a different state, and inference delay changes the prior for action generation.

These functions may eventually fit into one model, or they may live across several models and controllers. I am not especially concerned with where the parameters end up for now. As long as the world keeps moving, the relationship between slow planning and fast feedback still needs to be handled.

And finally, we return to the question we cannot get around: who tells the agent whether it got things right?

The lesson from SWE agents is straightforward. At first, a person pasted the code into an environment, ran it, and took the errors back to the model. Later, the agent ran the code, read the errors, and made changes itself. That loop is where many of its abilities became useful. Could a physical agent have a similar verifier? I think it should, although that need not mean adding another large model.

Executing code does not itself guarantee that the requirements have been met. A normal exit code and passing tests cover only certain properties. With robots, the distinction is even more obvious: reaching the target pose does not mean an object was grasped; a closed gripper does not mean it has not slipped away; a plug that looks inserted does not mean there is an electrical connection.

GPT-6's improved judgment of spatial state will clearly alleviate many problems. But the RoboDojo report contains another detail: the model notices that the simulator has not terminated and starts to suspect that some condition remains unmet. That is a good use of feedback, but it also means the environment is still telling it “not successful yet.” Whether it can judge completion just as reliably with that signal removed needs a separate test.

Thea already tries an [independent evaluation step](https://eit-hai.github.io/thea/documentation/evaluation/index.html), judging the result from fresh observations after execution and the tool's postconditions. I think separating evaluation from the acting model's own report is useful. Otherwise, after investing so much effort in its plan, the model may read an ambiguous image through the assumption that “this should have worked by now.”

But two models looking at the same heavily occluded image may also guess wrong together. Sometimes there simply is not enough information. Is the object pinned down? Is the contact surface slipping? Is the plug conducting electricity? The current image alone may not distinguish these possibilities. A larger model does not automatically produce the missing evidence.

What is needed might be another viewing angle, a force reading, an electrical continuity check, or a gentle motion to see whether the object actually moves with the gripper. The most useful part of independent verification is often another source or angle of evidence, rather than another judgment with the same priors.

To push the idea further, verification could participate in planning from the start.

Suppose two routes both put an object into place. One is fastest but leaves the object completely hidden. The other takes a small detour but leaves the relevant part visible afterward. If we compare only whether the motion succeeds, the two may look similar. With many steps still to come, however, the second may be a better choice: it is easier to verify and easier to recover from if something goes wrong.

Consider the earlier example of “nudging an object into a position familiar to the VLA.” That might also put it somewhere easier to observe. Acting and judging are connected here. The upper-level planner could consider how the result will be checked and how a failure will be cleaned up while deciding what to do. A fairly direct experiment would keep the task goal fixed while varying how observable the outcomes of different routes are. Does the agent actively choose the route whose result is easier to verify? And does that actually improve the success of the tasks that follow?

Simulator ground truth should still be kept. It is useful as a training signal and for checking how accurate the verifier is. But if the eventual aim is real-world deployment, there should be experiments where ground truth is unavailable at runtime. Even implicit feedback such as “the simulator did not exit after I declared completion” needs to be controlled.

We could first hold the upper-level model and executor fixed, and compare ground truth, the upper-level model's own judgment, independent visual judgment, and judgment supplemented by additional sensing or active observation. Then replace the paused simulation with one that runs continuously. That should make it easier to tell whether the system lacks understanding, observational evidence, or time to react.

The evaluations themselves could use some fresh ideas. [What Are We Actually Benchmarking in Robot Manipulation?](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.04233v1) gets very high scores on some LIBERO subsets using DINOv2, an MLP, and task IDs. The result reminds us that high scores may not be testing language understanding. Here is the provocative version: training an action-sequence classifier with prior knowledge of the task seems to work rather well on these early benchmarks. So what is the language backbone actually doing? Are all those capabilities learned in the digital world really being put to use?

The same problem applies to the agents we want to build. Making a fixed workflow longer does not necessarily test autonomy. The harder questions are what happens when the goal changes in the same scene, a tool suddenly becomes unavailable, the previous action leaves things in a bad state, or a person changes the request halfway through. Can the agent still carry on and finish the job?

So I still broadly agree that “we may not need to build a general-purpose brain from scratch.” As for whether that leaves no room to train the upper level for embodiment, I think it is far too early to say. When to trust a tool, when to admit the evidence is insufficient, and when to abandon a half-formed plan could all be training objectives. Robotics researchers also need to think about the large-model side: how should the data be introduced, and which objectives make sense? Would pretraining simply mean throwing vast quantities of egocentric data into a fill-in-the-middle objective again? What, exactly, would we ask it to fill in?

Even if the upper level is expensive for now, as long as it actually does the job well, we can use it to collect intervention and recovery data, then give the common cases to cheaper policies and call it back for the hard ones. Whether that becomes a process of continual improvement depends on reliable feedback. If action generation and action scoring just keep agreeing with each other, there is still an external check missing before we can say anything has improved.

What we want to see now, more than another demo, is a robot doing hours of work that cannot be reduced to repeating the same routine. Running a marathon or sorting similarly sized parcels is a stress test; that is not what interests me most. GPT-6 brings the prospect closer to reality. Let us see how far it takes us. Meanwhile, I still find lightweight brain models built to meet real-time demands more compelling. I think the industry should pay more attention to Gemini's work in this area.
